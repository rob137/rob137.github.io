So there's, in Scott Adams's How to Fail at Everything and Still Win Big, there's this terrific anecdote early on about how he got on a plane and he sat next to the CEO who gives him one piece of advice, which is like, start looking for another job as soon as you've got a job. And there was always a part of me that thought this is fantastically good advice because you have no downside. So if you never find a better job, then that's useful information and very much affirming of your current position. But if you do find a better position, then that's information too, provided it's over the threshold where the loss of job security and the pain of switching roles makes it worth doing. You know, perhaps you should, unless there are very good reasons to do with loyalty, purpose, feeling at one with your team and so on to keep you where you are. If a job is merely a job and it's below the market rate, then of course you ought to move.

There are obvious things you might say about how that's not a very inspiring position, but I think there are also compelling cases to make around this idea that the corporate vision of companies as families is, you know, past the Dunbar number certainly, a bit of a fiction. And that all of us can tell many stories of either our own or our friend and family's experiences of being let go from jobs to which we were devoted. There is always the risk that your organization is just as psychopathic as the ones before it. Fine, all well and good, and the principal agent problem is in some ways boring, very well documented, and not worth any claps on the back for spotting. But how do you escape this loop?

I think the one answer that's being very naturally trudged up by AI at the moment is that you need smaller teams. Because actually, that Dunbar number I mentioned earlier probably is either itself a threshold or maybe something beneath it is. But there's a point where your company ceases to be a band of pirates and turns into something more amorphous, faceless, shifting, emergent, a corporation, something that you have no right to be appalled by if it turns self-interested in spite of your loyalty. AI makes the communications overhead between humans, as I've mentioned in previous blog posts, a massive, massive deal. Because it's a stubborn bottleneck that scaling laws can't really touch very well. But then also, if you can do more with fewer people, and if changing your mind is easier with a smaller band, then there are all sorts of business-related incentives to keep your team small, particularly if the tech isn't staying still, and it certainly isn't. In some sense, the Kent Beck idea that you might hyperlink of explore, exploit, extract is being subverted. If you can stay in the explore phase right now, I think you're actually a lot better off, which is historically pretty unprecedented, but these are very much weird times.

And then thirdly, without seeming too sickening, the human touch stuff, which was always there, is now clearer than ever because the rest is getting eroded down. If your founders really care about their craft, really care about their field, and still take care over making the business work well, while at the same time paying very close attention to the personalities, the interests, the needs, the foibles, the special intensity of each person working under them, then you can end up in a position very easily where deliberately ambiguous plans over a 6 to 12 month scale could be completely tolerable and which won't frighten the horses at all. In fact, if your team is sufficiently AI-pilled, it might even be somewhat of a filter for the right people, since anyone who's too headstrong in their predictions of how the world's going to be over those timeframes is probably no longer a good hire.
