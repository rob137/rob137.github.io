[The last post](/2026/10/13/the-hedge/) ended on Dennis Nedry, the programmer in Jurassic Park who feels underused and underpaid and sells embryos out the back door. Rereading it, I can see how someone at my own company might take that as a thinly veiled threat, which is the opposite of what I meant. I love the place, and part of the reason is that the founders take an unusually active interest in what each person is after: their motivations, their goals, what makes them tick. What I was gesturing at is a general problem, and I think small companies now have an unusually good answer to it.

Nedry is a cartoon, but he works as a character because you can feel his motivations even if you can't imagine selling DNA to a rival. Alienation from your work and a sense of being undervalued lead most people somewhere much duller than corporate espionage. They start fishing for a new job, and trade up against their best alternative offer, which is the job they already have.

Large companies mostly get away with ignoring this, for the same reason banks do. Your current account pays a poor rate once the introductory offer ends, because most people can't be bothered to shop around. Money Saving Expert exists because it has to: people need a mailing list to spur them into doing anything, since the default is doing nothing, and most of us let our arrangements quietly rot. Employment works the same way. The most loyal people in a business, often the most capable too, are quite often the most underpaid, because the numbers reward the company for letting inertia do the work. That has always made me uncomfortable, though it has never been a secret.

Scott Adams tells a story early in How to Fail at Almost Everything and Still Win Big about sitting next to a CEO on a plane. The advice he got, roughly, was that the day you start a job is the day you start looking for the next one. Part of me has always thought this is very good advice, because the downside is small. If you never find anything better, that's useful information and rather affirming of where you are. If you do, that's information too, provided the gap is big enough to be worth the lost security and the pain of moving. Unless there are good reasons to stay, to do with loyalty, purpose or feeling at one with your team, a job that is merely a job and pays below the market rate is one you ought to leave.

As positions go it's an uninspiring one, but the alternative story, the company as a family, is a bit of a fiction once you're past the [Dunbar number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunbar%27s_number). Most of us can tell stories, our own or our friends', of being let go from jobs we were devoted to. Your organisation may turn out just as psychopathic as the last one. The principal-agent problem is old and well documented, and nobody earns a pat on the back for spotting it. The interesting question is how you get out of the loop.

The answer AI is dragging into view is smaller teams. Somewhere around Dunbar's number, or probably well below it, a company stops being a band of pirates and turns into something amorphous, faceless and emergent, a corporation, and you lose any right to be appalled when it acts in its own interest regardless of your loyalty. Small crews stay on the right side of that line, and there are now hard business reasons to stay small. The first is the one I wrote about in [Brooks' Law Redux](/2026/01/03/brooks-law-redux/): communication between humans is a stubborn bottleneck that scaling laws barely touch, so its cost rises as everything else gets cheaper. The second is that if you can do more with fewer people, a small band can also change its mind more easily, and that matters a great deal while the technology refuses to sit still.

The second point upends something I used to take for granted. Kent Beck describes products moving through explore, expand and extract, and the usual assumption is that you leave exploring behind as fast as you can. Right now I think you may be better off staying in the explore phase for longer than would ever have made sense before. That's historically pretty unusual, but these are weird times.

The third reason is the human one, and I'll try not to make it sickening. The human side of running a company was always there, and it shows up more clearly now that so much else is being worn away. If founders care about their craft and their field, take care over making the business work, and also pay close attention to the personalities, interests, needs and foibles of each person working with them, then plans that are deliberately ambiguous over six to twelve months become perfectly tolerable, and the horses stay calm. If the team is sufficiently AI-pilled, that ambiguity may even work as a filter, since anyone too confident about how the world will look over those timeframes is probably no longer a good hire.

So the Nedry passage was about the hedge rather than about me. A company that keeps its options open has to keep its people close too, and a small crew whose founders know each person well is about the best arrangement I've seen for doing both.
