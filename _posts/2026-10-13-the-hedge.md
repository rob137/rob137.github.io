The snide joke you'll hear about forward-deployed engineers is that they're a hedge. Your software isn't general enough to hand to any customer who turns up, so you parachute in someone who can smooth the difference. I said as much in a talk at work last week, and I meant it as a joke against the model. I'm less sure now which way round the joke goes.

The default destination for a small company with a product is software as a service. You build the thing, you make it general, customers log in and pay by the seat, and the consultancy work that paid the bills along the way gets quietly retired as a phase you grew out of. That assumption grew up in a particular era, twenty-odd years of cheap money in which venture capital could afford to fund the long climb to a general product. We are fish who have been swimming in that water for a long time, and it's worth noticing that it is water.

Geoffrey Huntley [posted this morning](https://ghuntley.com/agency/) about what he calls infinite software. Take an existing application, have an agent turn it into a specification, then have another agent rebuild it from the specification. He says he has just rebuilt the products of three of his former employers in under ten days, and plans to give them away. His conclusion is blunt: if you're selling software without infrastructure or some other lock-in, you're hosed. I largely agree. The last few weeks have been full of people decompiling games and splicing one into another, a whole physics engine lifted out and dropped somewhere new by a private individual with a few prompts. Months ago a founder from a company in our building gave us some friendly advice: never let a customer get hold of your binaries, keep everything behind a web interface and an API. It sounded slightly paranoid at the time. Now it sounds like the minimum, and even that only protects the code.

So what can't be rebuilt in a weekend? Huntley's answer, from the same post, is that people now sit at two points: the vision at the start and the verification at the end, with the implementation in between turning into a black box. That is a fairly exact description of what a good forward-deployed engineer does at a customer site. They decide what the customer actually needs, which is rarely what was asked for, and they know which results are wrong. They also carry the accountability, the name on the drawing when someone senior asks who approved it. None of that is in the binary, so none of it can be decompiled.

Then there's the money. I can't shake a feeling that AI infrastructure spending has a whiff of 2008 about it rather than 2000. The worry isn't that the technology is a fad, because it plainly isn't. It's the spending and leverage stacked on top of it. If something gives, the companies that suffer are the ones whose valuations assumed the music would keep playing. A small firm that pays its own way from work customers already value is about as well placed as anyone to ride that out. Huntley makes a related point: many of the eye-watering valuations aren't really about the product at all, they're bets on a team that works well together and might be bought. A team like that, with real depth in a domain, is worth something whichever way the market goes.

What a hedge doesn't do is make the risk go away. It changes the risk's shape, and the new shapes are easier to miss because they look like doing the sensible thing.

The first new shape is being displaced from below. Huntley also wrote recently about JetBrains [Kodaking itself](https://ghuntley.com/kodak/), optimising the existing product while the category underneath it was being displaced. A consultancy can do the same. If customers' own engineers, with their own agents, start doing the judgement work themselves, the person you parachute in becomes the thing that gets displaced. The honest version of the hedge comes with a question you keep asking: is the part we sell still the part they can't do themselves?

The second shape is human, and it doesn't show up on a spreadsheet. In Jurassic Park, Dennis Nedry is the programmer who builds the whole system, feels underused and underpaid, and ends up selling embryos out the back door. It's a cartoon, but the shape is real. A company that keeps its options open is often doing the right thing for the balance sheet while asking its most restless people to idle. The people who see furthest are the ones most likely to drift towards outside interests when the interesting bet keeps getting deferred. If you're going to hedge, it's worth saying so out loud, so that waiting is a decision everyone has heard rather than a vacuum people fill with their own stories.

I started out thinking of forward-deployed engineering as scaffolding, the thing you do until the software is finished. I'm no longer sure the software is ever finished in that sense. If it's being regenerated continuously, by you and by anyone else who fancies it, then the person who smooths the difference between the software and the customer might be the product, and the hedge might be the bet.
