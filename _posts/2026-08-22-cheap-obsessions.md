For the last few days I have been learning far too much about bolt cutters.

I started with a fairly ordinary purchasing question. Knipex make several versions of their CoBolt cutters. There is a 200mm version, a 250mm version, angled jaws, straight jaws, recessed cutting edges, different handles. I wanted to know whether one was simply better, or whether the differences were real.

I asked what electricians thought. Then mechanics. Then people breaking up pallets, because I reclaim quite a lot of wood and spend more time than I would like dealing with nails and screws left behind. I went looking through German reviews, then French ones, Russian ones, Japanese ones, Chinese ones. Whenever the answers started converging, I tried another direction.

Eventually the annoying answer emerged. The trade-offs were mostly real. The 250mm cutters have more leverage. The 200mm ones fit into places the larger pair don't. Angled jaws solve one sort of access problem while creating another. End-cutting nippers do something neither does particularly well. If you actually want full coverage there is no clever choice that makes the other tools disappear.

This is perhaps how I have ended up spending an embarrassing amount on cutters over the last few days.

I have [written about my affection for pliers before](/2026/01/03/pliers/), so none of this is entirely out of character. What interested me this time was the process. I am now absurdly well informed about a small category of hand tools that a week ago I barely understood, and I didn't sit down and decide to study them. Most of this happened while walking the dogs, talking into my phone.

A rabbit hole used to have quite a high entry price. I might wonder whether the angled CoBolt had some hidden disadvantage, search Google, open a few reviews and eventually run out of patience. Looking for opinions from Russian tradesmen would involve finding the right words to search for, translating pages and working out whether the person writing knew what they were talking about. At some point the original question would stop being worth the effort.

Now I can say "try Russia" while walking down the road.

Earlier I wrote about [prompts getting shorter through repeated use](/2026/08/21/knees-out/). Voice adds another part to this. The cost of asking the next question can get remarkably close to the cost of having the thought.

That creates a slightly dangerous flywheel. Most prompts produce another small piece of confirmation. Every so often one turns up something genuinely interesting. Somebody uses the tool differently. A complaint turns out to depend on a particular cutting geometry. A trade I hadn't considered has a completely different reason for preferring the smaller tool. The occasional surprise makes another prompt very tempting. The whole process has a slot-machine quality to it.

I have always been fairly sympathetic to rabbit holes anyway. We tend to treat them as failures of discipline because, historically, they consumed the same hours as whatever we were supposed to be doing. If you are meant to be writing a report and instead spend the afternoon reading about Japanese saws, the saws have taken the afternoon.

But I increasingly think of an obsession as a net. You throw it into some patch of the world because you are trying to catch one particular thing, and if you pull it through enough material other things come back with it.

In this case I came back with a better sense of metallurgy, mechanical advantage, cutting geometry, fasteners, tool manufacturing and the way several trades approach the same physical problem. Some of what came back wasn't about tools at all.

I started noticing why certain purchases hook me and others don't. Better cutters attach themselves to recurring jobs I already do, whereas specialist electronics or fencing pliers leave me fairly cold until there is an actual project. I also got to watch useful research turn into an urge to optimise towards complete coverage, and ask what would actually count as enough. The consumer loop became part of the thing I was examining rather than just the means by which I bought more pliers.

None of that was in the original question. The question only had to get the net into the water.

LLMs change the economics because they let you pull the net much faster. They can translate, explain terminology, search another country, compare competing claims and suggest another direction while I contribute little more than curiosity. I can get surprisingly deep into a subject before the effort involved starts pushing me back out.

That makes me wonder whether I should be cultivating some obsessions rather than trying to suppress them.

There is a shadow side. I was talking about this with a friend this morning in a different context, and he pointed out a standard failure mode: spending months tinkering with product ideas and enjoying yourself enormously while believing you are making progress. The activity feels productive because it contains research, decisions, prototypes and lots of clever conversation.

Cheap exploration also makes cheap pseudo-progress possible. The pliers version is fairly harmless. In other domains the distinction between productive exploration and elaborate avoidance matters much more, and the mechanism has no idea which one you are doing.

Something related is already happening at work. I wrote in January about becoming an [orchestrator](/2026/01/21/orchestrator/), with coding agents doing work I would previously have done myself. I can now have several terminal windows open with different tasks running in them. A few years ago that would have been a reasonable description of being hopelessly distracted. Now some of those windows contain agents that keep working after I move elsewhere.

The job increasingly involves starting something, switching away, coming back to inspect it, redirecting one thread and abandoning another while the rest continue without me. Managers have always worked a little like this because they aren't personally executing every step. Agents are beginning to give the rest of us some of the same economics.

That makes some cognitive habits look slightly different. Rapid task switching, following tangents and keeping several threads alive at once are not universally useful, but they are less obviously pathological when execution can continue after your attention has moved elsewhere.

Our culture of attention developed in a world where investigation and execution competed for the same scarce human time. Following a tangent generally meant stopping the useful thing you were doing. Now the next question might cost a sentence spoken into a phone while walking the dogs.

It doesn't make attention abundant, but it does make curiosity cheaper. Temporary obsession becomes a more practical way of learning about the world because you can go deep enough to acquire the vocabulary, distinctions and odd bits of knowledge that normally only appear after prolonged exposure, then move on.

I don't think this means focus is obsolete. Some things still require long periods of uninterrupted attention, and LLMs are capable of becoming an extraordinarily sophisticated form of procrastination. But I am less sure that distraction and exploration are the same category.

Perhaps we have spent so long protecting attention from rabbit holes that we have underpriced what rabbit holes produce. Their value was partly hidden by how expensive they were.

I have almost certainly bought enough pliers now. The more interesting thing I got from the exercise was a demonstration of how far it is suddenly possible to wander into an obscure subject and come back out again, dragging a net full of things I hadn't gone looking for.

We have spent a long time learning how not to get distracted. I suspect we are going to have to learn when to get distracted on purpose.
